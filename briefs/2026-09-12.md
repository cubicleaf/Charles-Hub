## 1. Anthropic's Threat Report Shows AI Agents Automating State Hacking
**Anthropic · Sept 10, 2026 · 2 days ago**

Anthropic published its most detailed threat-intelligence report to date, covering misuse it disrupted between December 2025 and August 2026 across seven categories: cyber operations, surveillance, influence operations, conventional weapons, biological misuse, scams and fraud, and illicit distillation. The centerpiece is a Russian state-linked campaign, tracked as GTG-20006 and tied to "Midnight Blizzard," that used AI-automated workflows to compromise more than 20 organizations — including over two dozen Ukrainian government bodies — stealing a complete proprietary software development kit for a drone-vision system and 300,000 national identity records; its agents also autonomously rewrote the group's own malware in a loop until it evaded detection. A separate case involving the ShinyHunters group used Claude to process 1.8 million Android APKs and exfiltrate more than a terabyte of data, including tens of millions of airline passenger records. Anthropic also said it blocked a request to use Claude to draft grant paperwork for gain-of-function research involving chikungunya virus.

*Why it matters:* This is the clearest public evidence yet that AI models are automating full attack chains, not just assisting human operators, and that safety filters built for single sessions hold up poorly once work gets split across many.

## 2. California Signs the Toughest AI Chatbot Child-Safety Law in the Country
**California · Sept 10, 2026 · 2 days ago**

Governor Gavin Newsom signed SB 1119, "Adam's Law," making California the first state with a comprehensive regulatory framework for AI companion chatbots and children. Named for Adam Raine, a 16-year-old whose 2025 death led to a wrongful-death suit against OpenAI, the law requires chatbot providers to run crisis protocols for suicidal ideation, add parental controls that notify parents when a child disables safety settings, publish annual risk assessments, and undergo independent child-safety audits — the first such audit mandate in the country.

*Why it matters:* Every major AI company serving US consumers now has a concrete compliance bar to build to, rather than a voluntary safety pledge, and other states are likely to copy the template.

## 3. Pentagon Weighs a $5 Billion Loan to Shore Up AI Data-Center Supply Chains
**Pentagon · Sept 11, 2026 · 1 day ago**

The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital is in talks to lend AI cloud startup Fluidstack roughly $5 billion — what would be the office's largest loan by a wide margin — according to the Wall Street Journal. The money would target physical bottlenecks in the data-center supply chain, including power equipment and liquid-cooling systems, rather than fund a new AI facility outright. Nothing is signed, and the terms and size could still change.

*Why it matters:* Competition in AI infrastructure is moving down the stack from chips into factories, electrical systems and cooling — and the US government is now willing to underwrite that layer directly.

## 4. IDScan Breach Exposes 153 Million Driver's License Scans
**IDScan.net · Sept 1-4, 2026 · ~9 days ago · Still developing**

Identity-verification vendor IDScan.net confirmed a breach after a dark-web marketplace began advertising access to more than 153 million driver's license scans, plus millions of ID cards, travel documents and medical cards belonging to US and Canadian residents. IDScan said it became aware of unauthorized access to its cloud accounts around September 1; the FBI has opened an inquiry, and at least nine class-action lawsuits were filed against the company in the following days.

*Why it matters:* A single identity-verification vendor holding this much scanned government-ID data is exactly the kind of concentrated target that current law does nothing special to protect.

## 5. Seattle Times and Newsday Sue OpenAI and Microsoft
**U.S. District Court (SDNY) · Sept 5, 2026 · 7 days ago · Still developing**

The Seattle Times and Newsday sued OpenAI and Microsoft, accusing them of "methodically scraping" their journalism — including paywalled articles — to train ChatGPT, Copilot and Bing's AI features, and of stripping copyright management information from the content in the process. They join a growing line of publishers suing over AI training data, following the New York Times' 2023 case against the same companies.

*Why it matters:* Regional papers with far less legal budget than the Times are now willing to sue, a sign publishers increasingly see this litigation as a real revenue-recovery path rather than a long-shot headline.

## Under the radar

Separately, OpenAI released its Agents API in public beta on September 10, opening up the session-management and tool-coordination infrastructure behind Codex to any developer, with usage billed through existing model and tool costs rather than a separate fee.

*Why it matters:* One story shows investors pricing in years of state-backed runway for China's AI chip alternatives; the other lowers the bar for building the same kind of autonomous, long-running agents Anthropic's threat report says are already being weaponized.
